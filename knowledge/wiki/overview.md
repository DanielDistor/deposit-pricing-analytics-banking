# Deposit Pricing: Overview

## What Is Deposit Pricing?

Deposit pricing is how banks set the interest rate (APY) they pay customers who hold savings accounts, CDs, and money market accounts. Banks take in deposits from savers, pool that money, and lend it out at higher interest rates. The difference between what they earn on loans and what they pay depositors is called the **net interest margin** — a primary source of bank profitability.

From a depositor's perspective, the deposit APY determines how fast their savings grow. From a bank's perspective, deposit pricing is a lever for attracting funding and managing margin.

## The Federal Reserve and the Fed Funds Rate

The Federal Reserve sets the **federal funds rate** — the overnight interest rate banks charge each other for short-term loans. This rate is the benchmark for the entire U.S. interest rate system:

- When the Fed raises rates → borrowing becomes more expensive → banks can charge more on loans
- When the Fed cuts rates → borrowing becomes cheaper → bank loan revenues fall

The federal funds rate does NOT directly set deposit rates. Banks choose independently how much to pay depositors. This discretion is where the deposit pricing story gets interesting.

## Rate Cycles

The Fed moves through three phases:
- **Hiking cycle**: Fed raises rates to cool inflation (e.g., 2022–2023: from 0% to 5.25%)
- **Cutting cycle**: Fed lowers rates to stimulate growth (e.g., 2024: from 5.25% to 4.25–4.50%)
- **Hold**: Fed holds steady while assessing economic conditions

## The Spread and Why It Matters

The **spread** is the gap between the Fed funds rate and what a bank pays on deposits:

```
Spread (bps) = (Fed Funds Rate − Bank Savings APY) × 100
```

A wider spread means the bank is keeping more of the Fed rate increase as profit and passing less to savers. During the 2022–2023 hiking cycle:
- Chase spread: ~5.24% (kept nearly all of the 5.25% hike)
- Marcus spread: ~1.0–1.5% (passed most of the hike to savers)

## Pass-Through Ratio

The **pass-through ratio** measures how much of a Fed rate change a bank passes to depositors:

```
Pass-Through % = (Change in Bank APY) / (Change in Fed Funds Rate) × 100
```

Example: Fed raised rates 5.25% (2022–2023). Marcus raised savings APY by ~4.0%. Pass-through ≈ 76%.

High pass-through = bank is sharing rate increases with savers.
Low pass-through = bank is keeping the spread as additional profit.

## Why This Matters to Savers

During the 2022–2023 hiking cycle, the Fed raised rates 525 basis points. A $100,000 depositor:
- At Chase (0.01% APY): earned ~$10/year
- At Marcus/Ally/SoFi (~4.50% APY): earned ~$4,500/year
- **Difference: ~$4,490/year** on the same $100,000

Choosing the right bank has a concrete dollar impact — not a small one.

## Key Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| APY | Annual Percentage Yield — the effective annual rate accounting for compounding |
| Basis Points (bps) | 1/100th of 1%; 100 bps = 1.00% |
| Spread | Difference between two rates (e.g., Fed rate minus deposit APY) |
| Pass-Through Ratio | % of Fed rate change passed on to depositors |
| FDIC Insurance | Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation — insures deposits up to $250,000 per depositor per bank |
| Federal Funds Rate | Overnight rate the Fed sets as its main monetary policy tool |
| FOMC | Federal Open Market Committee — the Fed body that sets rate policy |
| Net Interest Margin | Bank's spread between asset yields and deposit costs |
